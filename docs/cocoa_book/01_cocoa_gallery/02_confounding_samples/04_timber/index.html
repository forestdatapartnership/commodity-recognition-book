Forests and Timber

Degraded forest

Degraded forests are one of the most common sources of confusion when identifying cacao systems. From above, they can resemble cacao agroforestry because both feature scattered tall trees mixed with smaller vegetation layers. However, degraded forests usually show a more irregular canopy pattern, with variable tree heights and a patchy distribution of vegetation. Time-series imagery can reveal the progressive degradation process such as selective logging, regrowth, or fire scars, while cacao plantations tend to exhibit more deliberate spatial arrangements and consistent maintenance over time. The absence of clear access paths, farm boundaries, or nearby agricultural infrastructure also helps distinguish degraded forests from cacao farms.

Timber

Timber plantations can also be mistaken for cacao systems, especially in humid tropical areas where both are grown in structured rows. The tall stature and dense, uniform canopy of timber trees can resemble mature cacao from above. However, timber species typically have taller and more homogeneous crowns, which cast distinct, elongated shadows visible in high-resolution imagery. In addition, timber plantations follow a longer rotation cycle of establishment, growth, and harvest, patterns that are visible in time-series data and differ from the permanent nature of cacao agroforestry, where canopy cover remains relatively stable year to year.
