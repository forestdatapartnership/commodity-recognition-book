Trellised Crops

Some crops are grown using vertical support structures such as poles, wires, or trellises, which create regular and distinctive patterns in high-resolution satellite imagery. These crops can in some cases be confused with cacao when viewed from above, especially when intercropped or when their planting density is high. Recognizing the linear or grid-like arrangements, along with shadow patterns, is key to distinguishing them.

Black pepper

Black pepper cultivation is characterized by several distinct features. The vine is typically grown on poles or trees, resulting in a recognizable pattern of vertical structures. These poles or trees, often part of agroforestry systems, are interspersed with shade trees, creating a heterogeneous canopy cover. The structured arrangement of the poles or trees often forms clear and repeating planting patterns visible in imagery. Shadows cast by the supporting structures further enhance the visibility of these patterns, particularly in high-resolution imagery. Additionally, black pepper is frequently intercropped with coffee, this can be identified as the shorter and denser coffee plants contrast with the vertical growth of pepper vines.

Dragon fruit

Dragon fruit, also known as pitaya, is a perennial, epiphytic, vine-like cactus fruit tree that exhibits distinct features observable in remote sensing. Like black pepper, dragon fruit is typically grown on poles, resulting in a very clear and organized planting pattern. The vertical structure of the poles, along with the shadows they cast, serves as a key indicator that distinguishes dragon fruit plantations from cacao plantations in high-resolution imagery.

Passion Fruit

Passion fruit is commonly grown on trellises or wire supports stretched between wooden or metal posts. These installations form regular, linear patterns that are easily spotted in satellite imagery, especially when rows are aligned and evenly spaced. The canopy itself may appear relatively uniform and medium in height. The visible support structures and row spacing can help distinguish passion fruit plantations from cacao systems.
