Cash Crop Plantations

Plantation cash crops are widely grown across tropical regions and often share structural similarities with cacao systems. These crops are typically cultivated in organized rows or blocks, producing distinct spatial patterns that can closely resemble cacao plantations in high-resolution imagery. Many are managed intensively for commercial production, and their canopy density, spacing, and layout frequently cause confusion during visual interpretation and AI-based classification.

This section presents the most common plantation crops found in or near cacao-growing landscapes, outlining the visual indicators that help differentiate them from cacao systems.

Coffee

Coffee plantations can often be mistaken for cacao systems, especially in regions where both crops exist. In high-resolution imagery, coffee and cacao share similarities in canopy density and planting arrangement.

Key distinguishing features include:

  • Coffee is generally cultivated in higher-altitude and cooler zones than cacao, which prefers lowland humid tropical climates.
  • Coffee plants tend to form smaller, rounder crowns and a more uniform canopy, whereas cacao trees have broader, slightly irregular crowns under partial shade.
Tea

Tea plantations consist of densely pruned small shrubs arranged in continuous rows, creating highly uniform textures and a fine-grained pattern in imagery. In contrast, cacao systems show more vertical structure due to the presence of taller shade trees. Tea also tends to occupy cooler, highland areas and presents distinct harvesting paths visible from above, helping differentiate it from cacao.

Rubber

Rubber plantations feature tall, evenly spaced trees with dense and uniform canopies. From above, mature rubber stands can resemble cacao, especially during certain phenological stages. However, rubber trees are deciduous, shedding their leaves seasonally. This strong phenological signal (leaf loss and regrowth) clearly distinguishes them from evergreen cacao. The long, straight planting rows of rubber also contrast with the more mixed and shaded pattern of cacao agroforestry.

Banana

Banana and plantain systems can resemble cacao in their dense canopy and mixed cropping arrangements, especially when used as temporary shade for young cacao. In imagery, mature banana stands display a distinct star-shaped crown with broad, arching leaves. Their rapid growth and short productive cycle (3–4 years) create visible changes over time that help separate them from cacao’s long-lived perennial structure.

Coconut

Coconut palms are tall, slender trees with large, fan-shaped crowns forming regular star-like patterns in imagery. Although their appearance is generally distinctive, confusion can occur when coconuts are intercropped with cacao or when image resolution makes canopy details less clear. The wider spacing and long shadows of coconut palms are useful cues for distinguishing them from the lower, denser canopy of cacao plantations.

Oil Palm

Oil palm plantations consist of medium to tall trees with dense, symmetrical crowns arranged in precisely spaced grids. These plantations can resemble large-scale cacao systems, particularly during early establishment stages, before palms develop their full canopy. Mature oil palms exhibit highly regular star-like crown repetition, whereas cacao tends to have more irregular, shaded patterns with variable crown sizes.

Cashew

Cashew trees are medium-sized and typically planted in semi-regular, widely spaced layouts. Their broad, irregular crowns and heterogeneous spacing distinguish them from the denser, more uniform canopy of cacao. In smallholder settings, cashew may still be confused with cacao agroforestry, especially when intercropped or on uneven terrain. Observing crown size, spacing, and associated vegetation helps to clarify such cases.
