Cocoa Varieties

Overview

Cocoa is grown in several major varietal types, each with distinct traits that influence how trees grow and how plantations are managed. The two primary ancestral varieties, Criollo and Forastero, originated in the Amazon basin. Criollo is known for its fine flavor but is delicate and more difficult to cultivate. In contrast, Forastero is hardier and more resilient, which led to its widespread adoption in Spanish, Dutch, and Portuguese colonies across West Africa, Southeast Asia, and South America. Criollo, Forastero and Trinitario are three types of cocoa cultivated in most of the regions. However, Forastero variety does well in Indian agro-climatic conditions. In the 1700s, a major shift occurred in the Caribbean, where disease and storms devastated Criollo plantations. Farmers began planting Forastero to support the remaining trees, resulting in a hybrid variety known as Trinitario, which is now widely cultivated.

Cocoa trees can also differ based on how they are propagated:

  • Seed-grown trees tend to be larger, with broader canopies and more irregular spacing in the field.
  • Clonal varieties, which are vegetatively propagated, are smaller, more uniform in height, and typically grown in more organized planting systems, often using a 3×3 meter spacing grid.

These differences in size, structure, and layout can influence how cocoa fields appear in satellite imagery, particularly when distinguishing between traditional and more intensively managed systems.
