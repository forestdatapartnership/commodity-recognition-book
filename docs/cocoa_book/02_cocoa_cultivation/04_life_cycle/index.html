The Cocoa Life Cycle

Overview

Cocoa is a long-lived, perennial tree crop with a life cycle that can span several decades. Establishing a cocoa plantation requires careful early management, especially during the seedling stage, when trees are sensitive to light and moisture. Shade and sometimes irrigation are essential during this period, and cocoa is often interplanted with fast-growing crops like banana or cassava to provide early protection and income.

Cocoa trees typically become productive between two and five years after planting, depending on the variety. For instance, clonal types tend to fruit earlier and more uniformly than seed-grown trees. Once mature, trees remain productive for 20 to 25 years, although many are kept longer due to cultural preferences or economic constraints. As the trees mature, they produce pods that ripen throughout the year, changing color from dark green or purple to yellow, orange, or red, depending on the variety. This gradual change in canopy and pod coloration can affect how cocoa appears in satellite imagery, particularly in fields at different stages of maturity.
