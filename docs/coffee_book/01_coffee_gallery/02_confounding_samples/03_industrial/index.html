Cash Crops

Overview

Plantation cash crops are widely grown in tropical regions and often share structural similarities with coffee plantations. These crops are usually cultivated in organized rows or blocks, creating distinct spatial patterns that can closely resemble coffee in high-resolution imagery. Many are managed intensively for commercial production, and their canopy size, density, and layout make them frequent sources of confusion in visual interpretation and AI-based classification.

This section describes the visual characteristics of the most common plantation tree crops found in or near coffee-growing landscapes, highlighting the visual cues that help distinguish them from coffee. Those descriptions are followed by a series of visual examples.

Cocoa

Cocoa is often grown under partial shade, either in traditional agroforestry systems or in more organized plantations. The trees are small to medium in size and are typically planted in regular or semi-regular spacing. In high-resolution imagery, cocoa can look very similar to coffee.

Key distinguishing features include:

  • Slightly broader and more irregular crown shapes than coffee
  • More common in lower elevations and humid regions, especially in West Africa, Southeast Asia, and parts of Latin America

Tea

Tea plantations consist of small trees that are frequently pruned, as harvesting involves picking mature leaves. Tea is often cultivated within agroforestry systems, where existing shade trees are kept when developing the tea plantation. The tea plants are typically grown in dense, continuous rows, creating a uniform pattern that is distinctly visible in high-resolution imagery. This planting arrangement closely resembles that of pruned coffee, making it challenging to differentiate between the two crops when they are grown in the same area.

Rubber

Rubber cultivation is characterized by tall trees with a dense canopy, which is notably thicker than that of coffee plantations. The trees are typically planted in organized rows, resulting in a clear and structured planting pattern visible in high-resolution imagery. However, rubber plantations can sometimes resemble coffee cultivation due to similar planting arrangements. Additionally, rubber trees exhibit strong phenological changes as they are deciduous (i.e., they shed their leaves seasonally). This phenology can lead to confusion with coffee plantations, particularly during certain growth stages or when both crops are grown in close proximity.

Banana

Banana and plantain plantations can resemble cocoa and coffee during their growing phase due to their similar canopy structure. However, as the plants reach maturity, they develop a distinct star-shaped canopy pattern, which serves as a key identifier. These plants reach maturity within a year and remain productive for three to four years, making them a common choice for providing shade to young coffee plants. Additionally, bananas and plantains are often used as intercrops even after coffee plantations reach maturity.

Coconut

Coconut palms are tall trees with large, fan-shaped leaves that form a distinct star-like pattern in imagery. They are typically spaced farther apart than coffee trees, and their tall, slender trunks cast long shadows in imagery. While their crown shape is distinctive, coconut plantations can still be confused with shaded coffee or intercropped systems if spacing and shadowing are unclear.

Oil palm

Oil palm plantations consist of medium to tall trees with wide, dense crowns arranged in highly regular planting grids. These plantations are highly structured and can appear similar to large-scale coffee monocultures, especially when young. The mature palms have a symmetrical star-like shape, often occurring in repeating patterns. However, in low-contrast or early-growth imagery, distinguishing oil palm from coffee can still be challenging.

Cashew

Cashew trees are medium-sized and often planted in semi-regular or loosely spaced patterns. Their canopy is generally broader and more open than coffee, with irregular shapes that vary across farms. In smallholder systems, cashew may appear similar to scattered coffee or agroforestry systems, particularly when intercropped or growing on sloped terrain. However, the larger crown size and less uniform spacing can help in distinguishing them.
